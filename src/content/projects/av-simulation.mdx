## Overview

An autonomous-vehicle training simulation built on **NEAT** (NeuroEvolution of Augmenting
Topologies), a genetic algorithm that evolves both a neural network's topology and its
weights. Cars learn to drive 2D race tracks in a Pyglet simulation, and the work was
published at **ICORIS 2024**.

## The problem

Hand-designing a neural controller for driving — both its architecture and its weights — is
hard and brittle. Evolutionary methods sidestep this by letting the network *structure* and
its weights emerge together from selection pressure over generations. NEAT is well-suited
because it grows the simplest effective topology incrementally and preserves diverse
solutions via speciation, rather than fixing the architecture up front.

## Approach

Each car is a 2D object equipped with **distance sensors** (rays cast around the vehicle).
Those readings feed a neural network whose four outputs are **accelerate, brake, turn left,
and turn right**. NEAT evolves a population of these networks across generations: the
**fitness function counts the checkpoints (track gates) a car passes**, and a **30-second
timer** caps each generation so a single run can't stall the simulation. The best car of each
generation becomes the parent for the next, with crossover and mutation driving exploration.

I tested on three tracks of increasing difficulty — **Track A (33 checkpoints, easy), Track B
(37, medium), and Track C (46, hard)** — and ran a hyperparameter study over population size
and mutation rate to see how each configuration converges. Against the default (population 40,
mutation 0.6), I compared three tunings: **Tuning 1** (20 / 0.5), **Tuning 2** (60 / 0.8), and
**Tuning 3** (80 / 0.4).

## Results

**Tuning 2 (population 60, mutation rate 0.8) was the strongest overall**, rising fastest in
early generations and reaching peak fitness scores of **84, 98, and 100 on Tracks A, B, and
C** respectively. Across configurations the best scores landed around the **50th generation**
and didn't improve meaningfully past the 100th. The smaller population (Tuning 1) did
relatively well only on the easy track, while the large-population/low-mutation setup
(Tuning 3) progressed slowly early but caught up on the hardest track. The work was published
at the 2024 6th International Conference on Cybernetics and Intelligent System (ICORIS).

## What I learned

The headline finding is that **NEAT is genuinely sensitive to its hyperparameters** — because
it searches over topology *and* weights through random mutation, the right population/mutation
balance matters more than it would for a fixed-length-chromosome GA, and the best setting
shifts with track difficulty. That sensitivity is the main limitation to flag for future
work, and it's the throughline into the gradient-based RL I did later: NEAT discovers
controller *structure* well, but careful tuning is the price of that flexibility.
