## Overview

A multi-objective traffic signal controller that pairs **NSGA-II**, a genetic algorithm, with
an **LSTM-DQN** reinforcement learning agent. Instead of hand-picking how much throughput,
fairness and smoothness should matter, NSGA-II **evolves the reward weights** the agent trains
on, and returns a Pareto front of policies, each a different trade-off. The work was published
in **Procedia Computer Science** as part of the 11th International Conference on Computer
Science and Computational Intelligence 2026.

## The problem

Fixed-time control (Webster, 1958) runs a pre-set timer and breaks the moment traffic stops
matching history. Adaptive systems like **SCOOT and SCATS** react to live sensors but still
lean on calibrated models. Deep RL adapts in real time, but it inherited the single objective.
Real traffic is three goals pulling against each other: **throughput** (move the most cars),
**fairness** (no lane waits forever) and **smoothness** (avoid stop-and-go waves). The usual
fix is a hand-weighted sum like `0.7 · throughput + 0.2 · fairness + 0.1 · smoothness`, which
means guessing the weights and only ever exploring the one strategy they point to. An earlier
study (Leal, 2023) used NSGA-II for traffic too, but only to optimise static signal timings.

## Building it

The controller is a **two-level loop**. NSGA-II proposes a population of weight vectors
`[w1, w2, w3]` (normalised to sum to 1); for each one an LSTM-DQN agent is trained on the
reward `R = w1·r1 + w2·r2 + w3·r3`, run in the simulator, and scored on three fitness
objectives; NSGA-II uses those scores to evolve the next generation.

<ThesisLoop />

Everything runs in **SUMO** through **TraCI**, modelled on a real intersection: **BSD Raya
Utama × BSD Grand Boulevard** in BSD City, with traffic from four approaches and demand that
changes over time. The agent's **state** is the count of halting vehicles on each approach,
its **four actions** pick which approach gets the next green, and the LSTM gives it memory,
since a single snapshot never shows the whole picture. The rewards map onto the three goals:
`r1` cars cleared, `r2` the negative standard deviation of queue lengths, `r3` the negative
number of stops. The fitness objectives are **throughput, queue variance and total stops**.

NSGA-II ran with a **population of 30 over 20 generations**, binary tournament selection, SBX
crossover at 90% and polynomial mutation at 1/n. To make the agent train at all, I seeded the
replay buffer from a rule-based actuated controller and **rescaled the reward terms**, so jam
penalties didn't swamp every gradient. After a grid search: batch 256, learning rate 5e-4,
discount 0.95, gradient clipping at 1.0. Each policy was evaluated over 1,000 steps with a
10–60 s green window and a 6 s yellow; the 60 s cap stops a greedy agent holding one light
forever.

## Outcome

I compared the NSGA-II balanced policy against four baselines, and it was **best on all three
metrics at once**:

| Method | Throughput ↑ | Queue variance ↓ | Total stops ↓ |
|---|--:|--:|--:|
| Fixed-time | 1,011 | 67 | 35,913 |
| Rule-based actuated | 1,015 | 20 | 20,429 |
| RL, max throughput | 1,032 | 21 | 20,977 |
| RL, max fairness | 625 | 393 | 78,822 |
| **NSGA-II balanced** | **1,059** | **13** | **14,676** |

Against the best baseline numbers, that is **35% lower queue variance and 28% fewer stops**.
The single-objective agents show why: max-throughput barely beat a simple actuated controller
and stopped more often, while max-fairness switched phases so aggressively it caused gridlock.

The Pareto front shows how the weights trade off against each other:

| Part of the front | Throughput weight w1 | Throughput | Queue variance | Total stops |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Throughput-heavy | 0.60 or more | high | above 12.5 | over 15,200 |
| Balanced | 0.50–0.55 | about 1,050 | as low as 11.0 | about 14,400 |
| Smoothness-leaning | about 0.38 | **about 1,059, the highest** | the highest | – |

The surprise is the last row: **the highest throughput came from a policy that cared more
about smooth flow than about throughput**. Training took **about 2 hours on a high-end
laptop**, and inference afterwards is cheap. SUMO and network training are stochastic, so
exact numbers vary between runs, but the pattern over the baselines holds.

## What I learned

The headline lesson is that **chasing an objective directly is often the worst way to reach
it**: the policy that moved the most cars cared least about moving cars and most about smooth
flow. Single-objective RL has a ceiling, and sometimes a cliff. **Reward scaling** turned out
to be the difference between an agent that learned and one that didn't. And the Pareto front
was more useful than any single policy on it, since an operator can run a high-throughput
policy at rush hour and a smoother one off-peak. It's also the meeting point of my earlier
work: where AV Simulation used a genetic algorithm to evolve the controller itself, here
evolution shapes what a gradient-based RL agent is rewarded for. The next step is scaling from
one intersection to a network of coordinating agents (multi-agent RL), with the coordination
problem and compute bill that brings.
